LLM inference increasingly employs both weight and KV cache quantization. In such weight and KV cache (WKV) quantized models, the core GEMMs operate on FP activations and INT weights or KV tensors. Today's GPUs execute these operations by dequantizing INT operands on the fly and running the resulting FP×\times×FP product on Tensor Cores. Although this approach reduces memory traffic, it forfeits the compute- and energy-efficiency benefits of quantization. Prior FP-INT hardware also falls short because it mainly accelerates linear layers and leaves long-context attention on the conventional FP path. This paper presents FINISH, a hardware/software system that translates array-level FP-INT efficiency into system-level LLM speedup. First, we identify that some KV-quantized attention mappings associate quantization scales with the GEMM reduction dimension, preventing conventional FP-INT datapaths from factoring the scale out of the accumulation. FINISH resolves this problem by folding the scale into the FP input operand, enabling linear layers, QKTQK^TQKT, and PVPVPV to share a single native FP-INT datapath with only 3.41% GEMM-engine area overhead. Second, we co-design a decoupled GEMM engine and a high-bandwidth tensor-memory subsystem with a low-cost, restricted tensor interconnect to sustain high utilization of the denser FP-INT array. Third, we extend the open-source Vortex GPGPU into an end-to-end stack and evaluate four incremental design points to isolate the system-level contribution of each architectural feature. On a real FPGA prototype, FINISH achieves geometric-mean end-to-end speedups of 3.24×\times× in prefill and 5.93×\times× in decode over the linear-only FP-INT baseline, while reducing energy per token by 2.67×\times× and 8.72×\times×, respectively.

Review #1037A

Pre-Rebuttal Overall merit

3. Weak accept - Above acceptance threshold; merits outweigh weaknesses

Paper summary

This paper presents FINISH, an end-to-end hardware/software system for LLM inference with quantized weights and KV cache. The key compute-side contribution is a quantization-direction-reconfigurable FP-INT GEMM engine that enables native FP-INT execution for both linear layers and quantized attention. In particular, FINISH uses scale folding to support Q-ROW quantization and combines it with reconfigurable zero-point correction for different quantization directions. To sustain the higher compute density of the FP-INT engine, FINISH further introduces a specialized tensor-memory subsystem with dedicated tensor/accumulator storage, restricted high-bandwidth connectivity, and software support for aligned allocation and tile-major layouts. The complete system is implemented on top of the Vortex GPGPU and evaluated using both a real FPGA prototype and 28-nm synthesis.

Strengths

The paper addresses a practical limitation of WKV-quantized LLM inference, where INT weights or KV-cache tensors are typically dequantized before being processed by FP datapaths. In particular, the proposed scale-folding mechanism provides a clean way to support Q-ROW quantization while retaining the efficient FP-INT/integer-reduction datapath, instead of falling back to per-element dequantization followed by FP reduction.

Another major strength is the completeness of the system implementation. The paper goes beyond the FP-INT compute unit and integrates the tensor-memory subsystem, DMA engines, runtime support, tensor layouts, and LLM kernels into an end-to-end stack. The FPGA prototype and ASIC synthesis further strengthen the evaluation. The reported results show substantial latency and energy improvements while maintaining essentially unchanged model accuracy.

Weaknesses

My first concern is that the restricted HBM–TMEM connectivity relies heavily on regular access patterns and software-controlled data placement. This assumption is reasonable for the dense LLM workloads evaluated in the paper, but it may become problematic for MoE models, where expert activation is dynamic and highly imbalanced. Frequently accessed experts could cause traffic to concentrate on a subset of HBM pseudo-channels or TMEM banks, creating hotspots while other channels remain underutilized. The paper does not discuss how the proposed restricted connectivity behaves under such dynamically imbalanced expert workloads or whether this issue can be mitigated without restoring a more flexible interconnect.

My second concern is that the static LMEM/TMEM/ACC-MEM partition is insufficiently justified. FINISH adopts a fixed 1 MB / 512 KB / 512 KB configuration, but the paper does not provide a systematic design-space or sensitivity study explaining why this partition was selected or whether it remains effective across different model sizes or sequence lengths. Since functional partitioning may introduce capacity fragmentation or uneven resource utilization across different execution phases, the current configuration appears somewhat heuristic.

Soundness

3. Largely sound: the design and evaluation support the main claims, with minor weaknesses or missing experiments that don't threaten the core results.

Novelty and Expected Impact

3. Solid contribution: the idea is meaningfully new and addresses a problem the community cares about, with a reasonable chance of influencing future work.

Reviewer expertise

3. Somewhat familiar – Basic understanding; not my main area

Comments for authors

I find the compute-side design compelling. Extending native FP-INT execution from linear layers to both (QK^T) and (PV) is well motivated for WKV-quantized inference, and the Q-ROW scale-folding mechanism is particularly elegant. It addresses the case where the quantization scale varies along the reduction dimension, which otherwise prevents a single post-reduction scaling operation and would require falling back to per-element FP dequantization and FP reduction. The reconfigurable zero-point correction and operand-loading mechanisms complement this design well.

The end-to-end implementation is another important strength. The paper does not stop at the arithmetic unit, but also considers the operand-delivery bottleneck introduced by the denser FP-INT MXU and develops the corresponding tensor memory, DMA, layout, and runtime support. The FPGA implementation and ASIC synthesis make the evaluation substantially more convincing than a compute-array-only study.

My main concerns are related to the generality and design-space justification of the tensor-memory subsystem. First, the restricted HBM–TMEM connectivity essentially trades routing flexibility for higher bandwidth and lower interconnect cost, which is reasonable for regular dense GEMMs. However, MoE models introduce dynamically selected experts with potentially highly skewed access frequencies. If several frequently activated experts are mapped to a limited subset of HBM pseudo-channels or TMEM banks, the restricted connectivity may lead to localized hotspots and poor bandwidth utilization, while other channels remain idle. The current paper does not discuss this scenario. Although MoE is outside the workloads currently evaluated in the paper, I believe it would be useful for the authors to clarify the limitations of the proposed design under such dynamic workloads and discuss whether expert-aware placement, data remapping, replication, or other low-cost mechanisms could alleviate the imbalance.

Second, the static capacity allocation among LMEM, TMEM, and ACC MEM is not sufficiently motivated. FINISH partitions the 2 MB on-chip storage into 1 MB LMEM, 512 KB TMEM, and 512 KB ACC MEM, but the paper does not explain how this ratio was chosen. Different layers, batch sizes, and sequence lengths may impose different requirements on general-purpose local storage, tensor operand buffering, and accumulator storage. A fixed functional partition may therefore leave some resources underutilized while others become capacity constrained. A sensitivity study over different LMEM/TMEM/ACC-MEM ratios would help demonstrate the robustness of the chosen configuration.

Overall, I consider the paper technically sound and well implemented. These concerns mainly affect the generality and design-space justification of the tensor-memory subsystem rather than the correctness of the core FP-INT design. I therefore currently lean toward weak accept.

Questions for revision/rebuttal

  1. How sensitive is FINISH to the static LMEM/TMEM/ACC-MEM partition? Please explain how the 1 MB / 512 KB / 512 KB configuration was selected. If possible, please provide a sensitivity or design-space study with different TMEM and ACC-MEM capacities, and discuss whether the preferred partition changes with model size or sequence length.

  2. How does the restricted HBM–TMEM connectivity handle expert load imbalance in MoE models? Expert activation in MoE models is dynamic, and access frequencies can be highly skewed across experts. If several hot experts are mapped to a limited subset of HBM pseudo-channels or TMEM banks, could this create significant channel/bank hotspots and reduce aggregate bandwidth utilization?

Review #1037B

Pre-Rebuttal Overall merit

1. Reject - Fundamentally flawed; reject without hesitation

Paper summary

This paper presents FINISH, an end-to-end FP-INT inference system for LLMs with quantized weights and KV cache. FINISH introduces a quantization-direction-reconfigurable FP-INT GEMM engine that supports both linear layers and quantized attention, together with a dedicated tensor-memory subsystem and corresponding layout/runtime support. The system is implemented on Vortex and evaluated using FPGA measurements, ASIC synthesis, and several controlled hardware configurations.

Strengths

  1. The implementation and evaluation are substantial, including an end-to-end software/hardware stack, FPGA measurements, ASIC synthesis, and controlled C1–C4 design points.
  2. The Q-COL/Q-ROW formulation identifies a meaningful interaction between quantization direction and GEMM reduction, and the paper provides concrete hardware support for both mappings.

Weaknesses

  1. The motivation shows that attention accounts for an increasing fraction of FLOPs and argues that dequantizing INT operands before FP computation is inefficient. However, neither observation demonstrates that this remains a significant latency or energy bottleneck in an optimized WKV-attention implementation. The paper itself notes that fused kernels such as BitDecoding can hide much of the dequantization latency, but does not profile such implementations to quantify the remaining overhead. Moreover, the evaluated attention path materializes intermediate attention tensors in HBM. Profiling representative optimized WKV-attention implementations is therefore necessary to establish where the remaining latency/energy bottlenecks are and how much optimization headroom exists for native FP-INT attention.
  2. The evaluation lacks direct comparison with representative recent systems. C1–C4 are useful controlled ablations, but C2 and C3 are author-constructed baselines. The paper discusses optimized GPU low-bit KV execution and several recent FP-INT / quantized-attention accelerators, such as BitDecoding, FIGNA, FIGLUT, LUT Tensor Core, and AxCore, but does not provide direct comparisons with representative prior implementations. Consequently, the current results mainly demonstrate improvements over the authors' Vortex-based baselines, making it difficult to assess FINISH's advantage over existing optimized solutions.
  3. The core research insights and design rationale are difficult to identify from the presentation. The Introduction and Motivation mix workload trends, roofline analysis, quantization background, memory-system observations, runtime support, and implementation details. The two key architectural observations—the Q-COL/Q-ROW mismatch in Fig. 4 and the loss of FP-INT array-density benefits after memory/interconnect overhead in Fig. 5—are not clearly distilled early in the paper. As a result, it is difficult to distinguish the central research insights from the supporting engineering mechanisms used to build the complete system.

Soundness

2. Major weaknesses: significant gaps in the design or evaluation (e.g., missing baselines, unfair comparisons, unsupported conclusions) leave the key claims unconvincing.

Novelty and Expected Impact

2. Limited novelty or impact: the contribution is incremental, or addresses a narrow problem unlikely to interest much of the community.

Reviewer expertise

1. Expert – Deep, current research involvement in this area

Questions for revision/rebuttal

  1. Can the authors strengthen the Introduction and Motivation with profiling that establishes the remaining optimization headroom in current WKV-attention implementations? In particular, for a representative optimized low-bit KV-cache implementation such as BitDecoding, how much latency and energy are still spent on KV dequantization and FP attention after fusion and pipelining hide much of the dequantization overhead? This profiling would help establish why native FP-INT attention remains necessary.
  2. How dependent are the reported gains on the current materialized-attention execution model? The evaluated implementation materializes intermediate attention tensors in HBM; it is unclear how much benefit would remain under a fused attention schedule such as FlashAttention/FlashDecoding-style execution.
  3. Can the authors provide a comparison with representative recent accelerator designs, such as FIGNA, FIGLUT, LUT Tensor Core, and AxCore? The current C1–C4 configurations are useful internal ablations, but they do not establish how FINISH compares with prior accelerator designs in terms of performance, area efficiency, and energy efficiency under comparable workloads and precision settings.
  4. Can the authors more clearly organize the Introduction, Motivation, and Contributions around the core architectural insights? In my opinion, the paper is primarily driven by two observations: (1) the Q-COL/Q-ROW mismatch caused by quantization scales along the GEMM reduction dimension, and (2) the operand-delivery bottleneck of a denser FP-INT array. TMEM, DMA, layout, and runtime support then appear to be mechanisms derived from these two problems.

Review #1037C

Pre-Rebuttal Overall merit

2. Weak reject - Below acceptance threshold; some merits but not enough

Paper summary

The paper proposes an FP-INT GEMM unit that handles both quantization directions appearing in weight- and KV-quantized LLM inference, together with a dedicated tensor-memory path and the layout support it requires. Everything is built into Vortex and evaluated on an Alveo U55C FPGA. On Llama2-7B and Llama3-8B with SpinQuant W4A16KV4, the paper reports 3.24x prefill and 5.93x decode end-to-end speedup over a linear-only FP-INT baseline, and 2.67x and 8.72x lower energy per token.

Strengths

  1. A decoupled matrix unit integrated into a GPGPU together with its SIMT pipeline, memory hierarchy, runtime, and software stack is a meaningful system-level design point. Running full LLM inference on FPGA also makes the evaluation more complete than an isolated array-level study.
  2. The numerical analysis shows that Q-ROW does not introduce additional error relative to the GPU FP16 path, model accuracy is preserved, and the area breakdown makes the cost of the added hardware visible.
  3. For some KV-cache quantization mappings, the scale varies along the reduction dimension of probability-value attention, so a conventional FP-INT datapath cannot simply factor the scale out of the reduction.

Weaknesses

W1. The motivation of this paper is weak. The paper does not cleanly isolate the need for native Q-ROW attention or the proposed tensor-delivery path. The comparison between the linear-only FP-INT baseline and the naive WKV FP-INT design moves attention from an FP path with dequantization to the FP-INT MXU. Therefore, the exact dequantization overhead is unclear, and the resulting gain cannot be attributed specifically to quantization-direction support. The paper needs ablation results that identify where the claimed bottleneck actually comes from and quantify its impact.

W2. The paper lacks an ablation study of the system-level optimizations. Moving from the naive WKV FP-INT design to FINISH simultaneously adds tensor memory, wider and more direct tensor paths, specialized DMA, constrained allocation and layout, and fused layout conversion. Although the paper reports their aggregate benefit and separately accounts for layout overhead, it does not show which component drives the gain. A small per-component ablation would substantially strengthen the full-stack claim.

W3. Only SpinQuant W4A16KV4 is evaluated, although the paper motivates FINISH using several KV-quantization schemes with different formats. The paper also does not quantify the costs of padding and alignment, non-coherent synchronization, incremental KV-cache updates, or compatibility with paged KV caches. It is also unclear how the design would interact with fused attention kernels that avoid materializing intermediate tensors.

W4. The performance comparison is difficult to interpret. The FP TCU baseline has 256 MACs, whereas FINISH uses a 1024-MAC MXU, yet achieved utilization and memory bandwidth are not reported. Therefore, it is difficult to separate architectural improvements from the 4x difference in peak compute capability. In addition, under SpinQuant, query-key attention maps to Q-COL, whose arithmetic is compatible with a FIGNA-style datapath; FINISH additionally supports transposed operand loading. The paper should clarify why the linear-only FP-INT baseline cannot execute query-key attention on its FP-INT MXU rather than treating all attention as FP fallback.

More details: D1. The platform difference should be considered when designing and interpreting the experiments. Given the FPGA prototype has a very different compute-to-bandwidth balance from the GPU used in the motivation, it is necessary to reason about the experimental settings.

The FPGA prototype is substantially more bandwidth-rich relative to compute than the GPU used in the motivation. This can make decode more compute-bound and amplify the benefit of the larger MXU. A roofline analysis of the prototype, or a projection to a more GPU-like compute-to-bandwidth ratio, would make the transferability of the results clearer.

Soundness

2. Major weaknesses: significant gaps in the design or evaluation (e.g., missing baselines, unfair comparisons, unsupported conclusions) leave the key claims unconvincing.

Novelty and Expected Impact

2. Limited novelty or impact: the contribution is incremental, or addresses a narrow problem unlikely to interest much of the community.

Reviewer expertise

2. Knowledgeable – Solid understanding, but not active in the area

Questions for revision/rebuttal

  1. Can you provide a performance breakdown of the current design? The paper introduces several separate system-level optimizations, but aggregates their benefits in the final results. It would be helpful to show which components provide the performance improvement.

  2. Please report achieved MXU utilization and memory bandwidth for representative workloads. How much of the reported 63.9x GEMM speedup over the FP TCU baseline remains after accounting for the 4x difference in peak MAC count?

  3. For the batch-64, 32K-context experiments, were layers or kernels measured independently and then aggregated? If so, what capacity effects, data movement, synchronization, and scheduling overheads are excluded?

Review #1037D

Pre-Rebuttal Overall merit

2. Weak reject - Below acceptance threshold; some merits but not enough

Paper summary

This paper proposes FINISH, an end-to-end hardware/software system for LLM inference with both weight and KV-cache quantization. The key architectural contribution is a reconfigurable native FP-INT GEMM engine that supports both conventional linear layers and quantized attention GEMMs by handling different quantization directions and operand orientations. In particular, FINISH introduces a Q-ROW execution mode that folds per-group quantization scales into the FP input before prealignment, allowing QKᵀ and PV to execute on the same FP-INT datapath used for linear layers. To sustain the higher compute density, FINISH also introduces a dedicated tensor-memory path with partitioned TMEM, specialized DMA engines, restricted high-bandwidth connectivity, tile-major layouts, and runtime support. The design is implemented in the Vortex GPGPU stack and evaluated using FPGA measurements together with 28 nm synthesis.

Strengths

The paper addresses a real systems problem that is becoming increasingly important as both model weights and KV caches are quantized. The quantization-direction-aware FP-INT attention datapath is technically interesting, and the paper goes beyond array-level efficiency by explicitly addressing operand delivery, memory-system overhead, layout constraints, and software/runtime support. The evaluation is also relatively comprehensive in terms of context length and batch size, covering 1K–32K contexts and both prefill and decoding.

Weaknesses

The main end-to-end comparisons are primarily against internally constructed C1–C4 design points rather than directly against recent external FP-INT or quantized-attention accelerators under matched system assumptions. Some of the very large reported speedups, especially C1-to-C4, combine multiple factors including a denser FP-INT array, native attention support, and substantially increased tensor-memory bandwidth, making it difficult to attribute the gain to individual contributions. The paper also evaluates only one concrete WKV quantization scheme, SpinQuant W4A16KV4, despite claiming support for multiple KV quantization directions. Finally, the reported end-to-end latency and energy are obtained by summing per-kernel measurements rather than directly measuring complete wall-clock execution.

2. Major weaknesses: significant gaps in the design or evaluation (e.g., missing baselines, unfair comparisons, unsupported conclusions) leave the key claims unconvincing.

Novelty and Expected Impact

2. Limited novelty or impact: the contribution is incremental, or addresses a narrow problem unlikely to interest much of the community.

Reviewer expertise

2. Knowledgeable – Solid understanding, but not active in the area

Comments for authors

I found the overall system design technically strong. In particular, the paper correctly identifies that supporting only weight-side FP-INT execution is insufficient once KV-cache quantization becomes important, because attention GEMMs can require quantization metadata along different logical directions. The proposed Q-COL/Q-ROW reconfigurability and the scale-folding approach for Q-ROW execution provide a concrete way to execute linear layers, QKᵀ, and PV on a shared native FP-INT datapath rather than falling back to FP×FP attention. The design also addresses the memory-system consequences of increasing array-level compute density rather than assuming operand delivery can scale for free.

My first concern is the external baseline coverage. The evaluation relies heavily on four internally defined design points: an FP TCU baseline, a linear-only FP-INT design, a naive WKV FP-INT design, and the complete FINISH system. This controlled progression is useful for ablation, but it does not fully establish how FINISH compares against recent FP-INT, LUT-based, or quantized-attention accelerators at the system level. The paper cites FIGNA, ANDA, LUT Tensor Core, FIGLUT, AxCore, and BitDecoding, but the main end-to-end results do not provide a normalized comparison against these designs. Since one of the paper’s central claims is that prior FP-INT accelerators fail to translate array-level gains into end-to-end LLM speedup, a more direct comparison with representative recent designs would strengthen this claim substantially.

Relatedly, the very large C1-to-C4 speedups should be interpreted carefully. C1 and C4 differ not only in arithmetic format, but also in array dimensions, native attention support, and the memory subsystem feeding the GEMM engine. In particular, the tensor path increases HBM-to-on-chip transfer bandwidth from 64 B/cycle to 512 B/cycle while also replacing the FP tensor core with a much denser FP-INT MXU. Therefore, the C1-to-C4 result represents a full-system redesign rather than the isolated benefit of a specific mechanism. I found the C2-to-C3 and C3-to-C4 comparisons more informative because they better isolate native FP-INT attention and the tensor-memory system, respectively. The paper would benefit from emphasizing these incremental comparisons and providing a clearer decomposition of compute-density versus bandwidth contributions.

The quantization generality claim could also be better substantiated. Table I discusses multiple KV-cache quantization methods with different K/V quantization directions, including KIVI, KVQuant, ZipCache, WKVQuant, QuaRot, and SpinQuant, and the proposed datapath is designed to cover both Q-COL and Q-ROW mappings. However, the actual end-to-end evaluation uses only SpinQuant W4A16KV4. Because quantization-direction flexibility is a central contribution of FINISH, demonstrating at least one additional scheme with a different K/V direction combination would make the generality argument much stronger.

I also have a methodological concern regarding the “end-to-end” measurements. The paper states that end-to-end latency and energy are obtained by summing corresponding per-kernel measurements. This is useful for controlled architectural analysis, but it may not capture complete wall-clock effects such as kernel launch, synchronization, pipeline bubbles, runtime scheduling, or overlap across kernels. Since FINISH is presented as an end-to-end hardware/software system, I would like to see at least one directly measured full-inference wall-clock result to validate that the summed-kernel methodology accurately predicts real system behavior.

The numerical validation is a positive aspect of the work. The paper explicitly examines the effect of changing the order of scale application in Q-ROW execution, reports ULP error relative to FP64, and shows that the resulting neural-network accuracy remains close to the GPU reference. This is a more convincing treatment than simply assuming that the reordering is numerically harmless.

Finally, I view the hardware novelty as concentrated primarily in the quantization-direction-aware FP-INT attention datapath, especially Q-ROW scale folding and shared support for standard/transposed operand loading. The dedicated TMEM, cache-bypass path, specialized DMA, aligned allocation, and tile-major layout are sensible and important system techniques, but conceptually closer to established tensor-accelerator memory-system design principles. The paper would benefit from making this distinction more explicit when positioning its architectural novelty.

Questions for revision/rebuttal

Can the authors provide a direct or normalized comparison against recent external FP-INT or quantized-attention accelerators, such as FIGNA, AxCore, BitDecoding, FIGLUT, or LUT Tensor Core, under matched area, bandwidth, and workload assumptions?

Can the authors further decompose the reported performance gains into contributions from increased FP-INT compute density, native FP-INT attention support, higher tensor-memory bandwidth, and the layout/runtime optimizations? In particular, how much of the C1-to-C4 gain is attributable to the 8× increase in HBM-to-TMEM bandwidth?

Can the authors evaluate at least one additional KV-cache quantization scheme with a different K/V quantization-direction combination from SpinQuant, to demonstrate that the claimed Q-COL/Q-ROW generality works in practice rather than only by mapping analysis?

Can the authors provide at least one directly measured end-to-end wall-clock inference result and compare it with the sum-of-per-kernel estimate used in the paper? This would help quantify launch, synchronization, scheduling, and overlap effects.

How does the tensor-memory subsystem scale if multiple FP-INT MXUs or multiple cores are instantiated? Does the restricted HBM–TMEM connectivity remain efficient as the number of compute engines and memory ports increases?

The paper reports FPGA-measured latency/power but ASIC-synthesized area and array-efficiency metrics. Could the authors more explicitly separate which claims are directly measured on FPGA from which are ASIC projections, particularly when discussing end-to-end energy efficiency and TOPS/W?
